# Message Spine — Vegan Dog Food Brand (Fresh Run)

## Core truths

**Truth 1: The nutrition question is real — and it deserves a real answer, not reassurance.**
"Plant-based diets are fine for dogs" stated confidently is not enough. Owners who care enough to be asking the question are also skeptical enough to see through a confident assertion. The brand has to show how the nutrition is done: what standards are met, how protein completeness is achieved, why this formulation is different from a poorly designed one. The argument is won on specifics, not on confidence.

**Truth 2: Dogs can genuinely thrive on a well-formulated plant-based diet — but "well-formulated" is doing all the work.**
This is a meaningful distinction. Not all plant-based dog foods are equivalent. A well-formulated one meets AAFCO complete-and-balanced standards, accounts for amino acid profiles (taurine, L-carnitine), and uses protein sources with adequate bioavailability. The brand's claim is not "vegan food is fine" — it's "this specific formulation is nutritionally sound, and here's why."

**Truth 3: The dissonance is real, but it can't open the argument.**
Many dog owners who arrive at this product feel a genuine contradiction: they hold strong values about animal welfare, but feed their dog factory-farmed meat every day. That feeling is real and worth acknowledging. But opening the homepage with it — before nutrition trust is built — turns the argument into emotional pressure. The values appeal lands harder after the rational case is made. Lead with "your dog can thrive on this," not "you should feel bad about what you're feeding."

## Audience distinctions worth preserving

**Values-led owners** arrive already believing in the concept. Their barrier is nutrition trust. Once the nutrition case is made credibly, the purchase decision is easy. This audience is the easier convert — but only if the nutrition argument is specific enough to satisfy them.

**Health-led owners** arrive because their dog has allergies or sensitivities. They may not be vegan themselves. Their barrier is efficacy: will this actually resolve the problem? They need the nutrition case too, but they also need something about why plant-based removes the allergen trigger that meat-based food couldn't. This audience is harder to convert from the homepage alone — they may need a dedicated allergy/sensitivity page — but the homepage shouldn't lose them before they find it.

The nutrition-first argument order serves both. It doesn't require the homepage to split into two tracks.

## Native language to keep

From signal:
- "nutritionally complete"
- "complete and balanced"
- "thriving on"
- "food allergies" / "food sensitivities"
- "factory farmed"
- "rendered byproducts"
- "my dog won't eat it" (the objection, named and addressed)
- "feel good about what I'm feeding"
- "whole household"

Tensions to preserve:
- The tension between wanting to align your values and being unwilling to compromise your dog's health — this is the real internal conflict, not "ethics vs. taste"
- The tension between "I trust this brand" and "I don't know if I can trust this category" — these are separate trust problems

Language to drop:
- "guilt-free"
- "values-aligned"
- "feed with purpose"
- "conscious pet parenting"
- "planet-friendly"
- "sustainable pet food" (in the hero — save for the values section)
- "your fur baby deserves better"

## Claims to test later
- That the nutrition-first argument order outperforms the values-first order for conversion — this is a testable hypothesis
- That addressing palatability directly (with a guarantee or trial) materially improves conversion among skeptical owners
- That "well-formulated" as a differentiator resonates with owners who have previously tried a vegan food and had a bad experience

## What this should change downstream

**Page choices:** Homepage first, nutrition-led. A secondary allergy/sensitivity landing page is warranted for the health-led audience but belongs in a second pass.

**Proof choices:** Nutrition proof should be specific and credentialed where possible (AAFCO statement, formulation involvement by a veterinary nutritionist, amino acid profile). Palatability proof needs to be a real offer, not a claim. Values proof (sustainability data, sourcing story) belongs downstream in the page, not in the opening.

**Language choices:** Lead with "thrive" not "fine." Lead with "complete" not "safe." Avoid "guilt-free" throughout — it implies the owner should feel guilty, which is condescending. Let the values argument emerge from the facts rather than from emotional framing.
