# Page Argument Shape — Vegan Dog Food Brand Homepage (Fresh Run)

## Page
Homepage — nutrition-first opening

## What the visitor needs to understand first
That dogs can genuinely thrive on a well-formulated plant-based diet — and that the word "well-formulated" means something specific here, not just a marketing qualifier. The visitor needs to feel that the brand knows what it's doing nutritionally before anything else on the page is credible.

If the page opens with values or sustainability, visitors whose primary question is "will this hurt my dog?" will not get what they came for. They'll bounce or stay unconvinced.

## What needs to be earned next
Once the nutrition claim lands: the visitor needs to know their dog will actually eat it. Palatability is the second gatekeeping question. A brand that addresses this directly — with a guarantee, a trial, or real owner reports — earns disproportionate trust because most brands skip it or bury it.

After palatability: the values argument can land. By this point the visitor has already cleared their two rational objections. The emotional argument — "you're no longer supporting factory-farmed meat" — now adds to a purchase decision rather than asking for one before it's been earned.

After values: proof. Specific owner examples that show what thriving on this food actually looks like.

## What the argument order should be

1. **Nutrition claim** — plainly stated, then backed with specifics
2. **How the nutrition is done** — what standard, what protein sources, what makes this formulation different from a poorly designed one
3. **Palatability** — addressed directly as a real concern, not dismissed
4. **Values** — now that the rational case is made, the emotional case
5. **Proof** — owner examples with specificity (health-led and values-led if possible)
6. **CTA** — trial, subscription, or first order

## Section plan

**Section 1 — Nutrition claim (hero)**
Headline states the core claim plainly: your dog can thrive on this.
Sub-headline specifies: complete and balanced, [AAFCO standard or equivalent], formulated to meet full amino acid requirements.
No sustainability language here. No values language. Just the nutrition claim and what backs it.
CTA: try it / start here.

**Section 2 — How the nutrition is done**
Three or four specific points about formulation:
- What protein sources are used and why
- What standard the food meets (AAFCO complete and balanced vs. supplemental — this distinction matters)
- How amino acid completeness is addressed (taurine, L-carnitine if relevant)
- Whether a veterinary nutritionist was involved in formulation
This section is earned by the hero. It answers "okay, but how?" for the visitor who is already leaning in.
Keep this behavioral and specific, not a technical data dump.

**Section 3 — Palatability**
Name the concern directly: "What if my dog won't eat it?"
Offer something real: a money-back guarantee, a transition guide, a sample program.
Show that the brand has thought about this and has a real answer — not "most dogs love it!" but a concrete fallback if they don't.
This section is short but important. It removes the reason most people don't try.

**Section 4 — Values**
Now that rational trust is built: the dissonance argument.
What a dog eating conventional food is connected to — not graphically, not guilt-trippingly, but plainly.
What changes when they switch.
This section earns the emotional dimension of the purchase without leading with it.

**Section 5 — Proof**
Two or three owner stories with enough specificity to be evidence:
- A values-led owner: what they were worried about, how the dog responded, what they noticed after a few months
- A health-led owner: what the dog's problem was, what changed, whether the allergy/sensitivity improved
Stories should be scenario-level, not rating-level. Generic "great food, highly recommend" is noise.

**Section 6 — CTA**
Clean. One action. Repeat the core claim briefly.
Optional: transition guide offer or feeding calculator to reduce first-order friction.

## What sections would be unnecessary or harmful

**Sustainability stats in the hero** — environmental benefit data before nutrition trust is built asks the visitor to care about the planet before they know their dog will be okay. Wrong order.

**Ingredient glossary or full ingredient list on the homepage** — belongs on a product page or nutrition page. Too much detail for a first visit.

**Comparison table against meat-based brands** — positions the brand as an alternative rather than a standalone claim. Weakens the argument.

**Founder or team story** — save for an About page. Doesn't do trust work for a skeptical first-time visitor.

**Social media / UGC image wall** — decorative rather than evidential. Wastes page space that proof could use.

**DCM disclaimer section** — don't bring up a concern the visitor may not have had. Address DCM in FAQ or on the nutrition page for those who ask.

## Where trust should land
Section 2 (how the nutrition is done) and Section 3 (palatability). These two sections together are where the purchase decision is made or lost for most visitors. The hero opens the door; these sections walk through it.

## Where proof should land
Section 5, after the values section. By the time the visitor reaches proof, they already believe the product can work (nutrition) and that their dog will eat it (palatability) and they've processed what they're moving away from (values). The proof at that point confirms, rather than introduces.

## Drafting constraints
- The nutrition claim in the hero must be specific enough to be credible — not "nutritionally complete" as a vague assertion, but tied to a real standard
- AAFCO complete-and-balanced statement must be verified before publishing; if this claim cannot be made, the hero section needs to be rewritten
- The palatability section must offer something real — a guarantee or trial program — not just a claim; if that doesn't exist, the section should be honest about what is offered instead
- Do not use "guilt-free" anywhere
- Do not open the values section with statistics about factory farming — let the plain description do the work
- Proof section must use real owner stories; do not invent specifics
