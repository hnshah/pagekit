# Page Argument Shape — Me CRM (Problem-First Landing Page)

## What the visitor needs to understand first

That the problem they've experienced — going dark on a relationship that matters, reaching out without knowing where they left things — has a name and is a pattern, not a personal failure. Recognition before solution.

## What needs to be earned next

Product distinction: why the current system (inbox, spreadsheet, sales CRM) doesn't hold relationship state, and specifically what fails. This is an experiential argument, not a comparison table.

## What the argument order should be

1. Name the failure mode — recognition section (hero)
2. Show what current tools fail to track — why-the-gap section
3. Introduce what Me CRM tracks — product section (state card, digest, reminders)
4. Address maintenance doubt directly — adoption section
5. Trust floor — data, trial terms, pricing
6. CTA

## Section plan

**Section 1 — The failure mode (hero)**
Names the experience: you meant to follow up, you don't remember where you left things, the relationship has quietly drifted. No product name in the first sentence. Recognition before solution.

**Section 2 — Why your current system doesn't hold this**
Specific about what breaks structurally: email threads scatter context; spreadsheets require deliberate update effort that disappears under pressure; sales CRMs track deals, not relationship state. Not a competitor comparison — a structural explanation.

**Section 3 — What Me CRM tracks**
Introduces the product through the relationship state card: last contact, what was discussed, what you owe, next step. Names the weekly digest and reminder system as the mechanism preventing relationships from going dark. Specific features, not capabilities language.

**Section 4 — The maintenance question, answered**
Addresses adoption doubt directly: the weekly digest surfaces what needs attention without requiring the founder to remember to log in; the iOS app captures notes immediately after a conversation before context fades. Honest about what still requires effort (initial setup, consistent note-taking).

**Section 5 — Trust floor**
Data: contact data not sold, data export always available. Trial: 14 days, no credit card required. Pricing: free tier (25 relationships), Solo $19/month. Brief — trust floor only.

**Section 6 — CTA**
Start the trial. One action. Reinforce no-credit-card-required.

## What sections would be unnecessary or harmful

- A features grid or capabilities matrix — not a product features page
- A testimonials section — none available; placeholders would undermine the honest posture
- An integrations section — Gmail import belongs in Section 3; a full integrations list is downstream
- A "for teams" section — team plan exists but fragments the primary audience; a solo-founder page should not split paths
- A competitor comparison table — the distinction argument should be experiential, not tabular
- A founder story section — earns trust downstream; must not lead before the user's problem is named

## Where trust should land

Primary trust is in the precision of the problem description itself. Secondary trust: trial structure (no credit card) and data posture (no selling, export always available). These belong near the CTA.

## Where proof should land

- Section 3: the relationship state card proves the product does what it claims
- Section 4: the weekly digest and iOS app prove the adoption concern has a specific mechanism
- Section 5: 200-waitlist number as early-validation signal (not a usage proof)

## Drafting constraints

### Proof and scope constraints
- no bare outcome claims ("increase funding probability," "close deals faster") — no data to support these
- no unsourced quantitative modifiers (`most`, `usually`, `typically`, `often`, `many`) unless the brief supports them
- no external-credential descriptions beyond what the brief supports
- no outrun mechanism or evidence-base claims
- no sections not approved by this shape

### Anti-slop constraints (hard rules)
- no `Not X. Not Y.` heading or sentence pairs
- no `Not-X-but-Y` or `X-not-Y` rhetorical pivots unless they carry specific content the affirmation cannot
- no three parallel short sentences used for cadence (rule-of-three clustering)
- no sentence-start anaphora across three or more consecutive sentences
- no editorial voice narrating brand restraint, virtue or honesty
- no em-dashes in body copy (use a colon, comma, or parentheses instead)
- no `actually`, `additionally`, `testament`, `landscape`, `showcasing`, `genuinely`, `truly` as intensifiers
- no marketing-punchy one-liners as section enders
- no scare-quoted antithesis
- no copula-avoidance verbs (`serves as`, `boasts`, `features`, `showcases`, `delivers`, `empowers`)
- no signposting (`let's dive in`, `here's the thing`, `that's the deal`)
- no filler (`in order to`, `due to the fact that`)
- no significance inflation (`pivotal moment`, `critical juncture`, `the future of`)

### Inline verification flag
When the draft makes a product-specific claim not verified against briefs or ground truth, mark it inline:
```
*[verification flag: confirm X against Y before publishing]*
```

## Length and density check

Six sections is within range. Sections 1–2 should be short (recognition lands fast or not at all). Sections 3–4 carry the product argument and can be moderate length. Section 5 should be brief.
