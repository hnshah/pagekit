# Page Argument Shape — Personal CRM for Startup Founders Homepage

## Page
Homepage — first-pass draft

## What the visitor needs to understand first
The problem this product solves is context loss, not contact volume. Before the product is introduced, the visitor needs to recognize their own experience in the problem framing: "I know who matters. I've lost track of where I am with them."

If the page opens with the product before the problem lands, visitors will default to pattern-matching it against the CRMs they already know — and most of those are wrong fits.

## What needs to be earned next
Once the problem lands: the visitor needs to understand why the existing alternatives (sales CRMs, Notion databases, spreadsheets) don't solve this well. This earns the right to present the product as something different, not just something new.

After the distinction is made: the mechanism needs to show how the product actually maintains relationship context — what does "knowing where you are with someone" look like in practice? This must be shown through specific relationship type examples, not abstract feature descriptions.

After the mechanism: trust and proof. The maintenance objection must be addressed directly. Then real founder use cases to make it concrete.

## What the argument order should be

1. **Recognition** — name the specific experience of losing relationship context (not "stay organized," not "manage your network")
2. **Distinction** — this is not a sales CRM; name why the sales CRM model is wrong for this job
3. **Mechanism** — show what the product actually does: surfaces current relationship state, logs context without overhead, reminds before relationships go cold
4. **Relationship type fit** — show that it handles the types of relationships founders have (investors, advisors, customers, potential hires) without forcing them into a pipeline model
5. **Trust answer** — address the maintenance objection directly and honestly
6. **Proof** — specific founder use cases showing context-loss problems solved
7. **CTA** — sign up or early access

## Section plan

**Section 1 — Recognition (hero)**
Open with the specific pain, not the product name.
Lead line should name the experience: you know who your important contacts are; you've lost track of where you actually are with them.
Sub-headline orients to the product: a relationship tracker built around current state, not pipeline stages.
CTA: early access or sign up.

**Section 2 — The wrong tool problem**
Short, pointed. Sales CRMs model pipelines. Investor relationships don't have a close. Advisor relationships don't have a stage. This is the wrong frame for how founder relationships actually work.
No comparison table. One clear paragraph. This section should make founders who tried HubSpot for their investor contacts feel understood, not sold to.

**Section 3 — How it works (mechanism)**
Show three or four specific things the product does:
- Log what happened after a meeting in under a minute
- See at a glance who you haven't talked to in a while
- Get a reminder before a relationship goes cold
- Pull up context before a call so you're not starting from scratch
Keep this behavioral. Avoid feature names and UI jargon.

**Section 4 — Relationship types**
Show that the product understands the categories of relationships founders manage. Not as a feature list — as a recognition moment. "For the investor who's been warm but waiting. For the advisor you haven't checked in with in three months. For the customer who gave you the best product feedback you've had."
This section earns trust by demonstrating product empathy for founder-specific relationship patterns.

**Section 5 — The maintenance question**
Address the objection directly: "Every CRM I've tried became a graveyard." Name it. Then answer it honestly — either with a specific mechanism (email sync, quick-log, whatever is real) or with an honest scope statement ("this works if you log interactions once a week; it's not magic if you never open it"). No overpromising.

**Section 6 — Proof**
Two or three founder use cases with specificity. Not generic testimonials ("great tool, highly recommend"). Scenario-level: "Before a fundraise, [founder] needed to know which investor conversations were actually warm and which had gone cold. She used [product] to…". These should be real if possible. If not yet available, mark as placeholder.

**Section 7 — CTA**
Clean. One action. Repeat the central promise briefly. No secondary CTAs, no pricing tease, no newsletter ask.

## What sections would be unnecessary or harmful

**Integration list in the hero area** — leads with technical capability before trust is earned. Move to FAQs or a dedicated integrations page if needed.

**Pricing section** — premature at this stage, especially for an early-access product. Founders will ask; the CTA handles the next step.

**For teams / team CRM** — dilutes the personal relationship angle. Do not include.

**Feature comparison table** — turns the page into a procurement decision before the recognition and trust work is done. The sales CRM distinction can be made in one paragraph.

**Generic testimonial carousel** — if the testimonials aren't specific enough to be evidence (scenario-level, not rating-level), they add noise and subtract credibility.

**"Why us" section** — avoid mission statements and founding story in the first pass. The product argument should do the trust work.

## Where trust should land
Section 5 (the maintenance question). This is the primary trust burden and should be addressed explicitly, not assumed away. If the mechanism section (Section 3) does its job well, some trust is earned there too — but Section 5 is where the objection gets named and answered.

## Where proof should land
Section 6, after the mechanism and relationship type sections. Proof is more powerful after the visitor understands what the product does. Proof placed in the hero area before mechanism is understood tends to float — the visitor doesn't yet know what the testimonial is evidence of.

## Drafting constraints
- Do not invent founder names or company names for testimonials; mark as placeholder if real examples aren't available
- Do not claim specific time savings, response rate improvements, or quantitative outcomes without data
- The maintenance objection must be addressed honestly — do not make the product sound zero-effort if it requires some effort
- Avoid "your network is your most important asset" and variations — hollow and overused
- Keep Section 2 (the wrong tool problem) short; this is not an attack on competitors, it's a one-sentence orientation
- The hero lead line should produce recognition in the first read, not cleverness — test it by asking whether a founder with the problem would say "yes, that's exactly it"
