# Page Argument Shape — AI-First PM Tool Homepage

## Page
Homepage — maintenance-burden-first opening

## What the visitor needs to understand first
That the reason their last PM tool failed was not the tool — it was that keeping it accurate required ongoing work that was never assigned to anyone. Before the product is introduced, the visitor needs to recognize this pattern in their own experience. If the page opens with the AI capability, teams who have heard "AI PM" before will skip past it.

## What needs to be earned next
Once the maintenance burden lands: a clear statement of how existing AI PM tools leave this problem unsolved. The argument is not that other tools are bad — it is that bolting AI onto a system that still requires human updates does not close the maintenance gap. This distinction has to be made before the product is introduced, or the visitor has no reason to believe this product is different.

After the distinction: the specific AI claim. What does the AI read, what does it create, and how does project structure stay current without someone deciding to update it. This must be shown as specific behaviors, not as a capability assertion.

After the mechanism: the accuracy question, addressed directly. What happens when the AI gets something wrong. A correction model, not a perfection claim.

After trust: proof. Team cases where the board reflected reality without a manual pass.

## What the argument order should be

1. **The maintenance problem** — name why PM tools go stale; produce recognition
2. **Why AI bolt-ons do not solve it** — the structural gap remains; earn the right to make a different claim
3. **The AI claim** — specific behaviors: what the AI reads, what it creates, how structure stays current
4. **The correction model** — what happens when the AI is wrong; address the accuracy trust question directly
5. **Proof** — team scenarios with specificity
6. **CTA** — start a trial

## Section plan

**Section 1 — The maintenance problem (hero)**
Open with the recognition, not the product.
Lead line: the experience of a PM board that does not reflect what the team is actually doing.
Sub-headline: one sentence on what the product is — a PM tool where the AI maintains the board from conversation, so the team does not have to.
CTA: start trial / get access.

**Section 2 — Why existing AI PM tools leave the gap open**
One short section. AI assistance reduces the friction of individual updates but does not remove the decision: a human still determines what enters the PM tool, and the PM tool still gets stale when that human is unavailable or busy. The gap between conversation and board remains. This section earns the right to make a stronger claim.

**Section 3 — How the AI maintains project structure**
Four specific behaviors, described concretely:
- When a team member says they have finished something in a thread, the AI updates the corresponding task
- When a dependency is mentioned in conversation, the AI creates a blocker link
- When a new commitment is made in chat, the AI creates the task without being asked
- When the conversation signals a scope change, the AI flags it rather than silently updating

Each described as a behavior that can be observed, not as a feature named and listed.
*[Verification flag: all four behaviors must be confirmed against actual product capabilities before publishing.]*

**Section 4 — When the AI gets something wrong**
This section exists because not having it will cost the page the engineering team. Short, honest. The AI will sometimes extract a task from a message that was not meant as a task. When that happens: one-click dismissal, inline correction, or a review queue — whichever the product actually provides.
*[Verification flag: confirm correction model before publishing.]*
The point is not that errors do not happen. The point is that correction is fast and the error does not compound — the wrong task does not silently propagate through the board.

**Section 5 — Proof**
Two team cases with scenario-level specificity:
- An engineering team without a PM: what their board looked like before, what the AI-maintained board looks like, what changed in how the team works
- A PM on a growing team: what the manual update work looked like, how that changed, whether the AI-maintained status was reliable for stakeholder reviews

Placeholder until real cases are available. Mark unambiguously.

**Section 6 — CTA**
One action. Restate the core outcome: the board reflects what the team is doing.

## What sections would be unnecessary or harmful

**Feature list or comparison matrix** — turns the page into an evaluation document before the primary claim has landed. The features are evidence of the claim; they are not the claim.

**Integration list in the hero or early sections** — raises "does this replace Slack?" before the AI distinction is established. Integrations belong on a docs or features page.

**Testimonial carousel with generic quotes** — "great tool, our team loves it" does no proof work for the skeptical engineering team. Proof must be scenario-level or it is noise.

**Security and compliance credentials** — relevant for procurement; not for first contact with a small team. Save for a security page.

**Pricing section** — premature before trust is built.

**"How it compares to Jira"** — defensive positioning that prioritizes the incumbent over the argument.

## Where trust should land
Section 4 — the correction model. This is the gatekeeping trust question for engineering teams and it must be answered directly, not dismissed. Section 3 earns product credibility; Section 4 earns AI credibility.

## Where proof should land
Section 5, after the correction model. Proof read before the accuracy question is answered will raise the accuracy question. The argument has to close the accuracy gap first.

## Drafting constraints

### Proof and scope constraints
- No AI accuracy statistics without verified source data
- No claim that the AI handles a specific message type without confirming it against product capabilities
- No unsourced quantitative modifiers — "most", "usually", "typically" — unless source briefs support them
- No sections not approved by this shape

### Anti-slop constraints (hard rules)
- No `Not X. Not Y.` heading or sentence pairs
- No `Not-X-but-Y` or `X-not-Y` rhetorical pivots unless they carry specific content the affirmation cannot
- No three parallel short sentences used for cadence (rule-of-three clustering)
- No sentence-start anaphora across three or more consecutive sentences
- No editorial voice narrating brand restraint, virtue or honesty
- No more than one em-dash per paragraph
- No `actually`, `additionally`, `testament`, `landscape`, `showcasing`, `genuinely`, `truly` as intensifiers
- No marketing-punchy one-liners as section enders
- No scare-quoted antithesis
- No copula-avoidance verbs (`serves as`, `boasts`, `features`, `showcases`, `delivers`, `empowers`)
- No signposting (`let's dive in`, `here's the thing`, `that's the deal`)
- No filler (`in order to`, `due to the fact that`)
- No significance inflation (`pivotal moment`, `critical juncture`, `the future of`)

When the draft makes a product-specific claim not verified against briefs or ground truth, mark it:
