# Raw Output 01 — Signal Doc

## Model
Claude (Claude Code, Opus 4.6)

## Raw output (as produced)

## 1. What the product appears to be
A plant-based dry kibble for adult dogs, sold as a monthly subscription. The product is positioned by its makers as a nutritionally-audited adult-maintenance food formulated by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist, with third-party amino acid testing. It is not a lifestyle brand, not a therapeutic food, and not suitable for puppies or medically restricted diets at launch.

## 2. Likely target audience(s)
- adult dog owners who are themselves vegan or vegetarian and have already looked into plant-based dog food
- specifically, the subset who bounced off the category because the trust evidence felt soft
- secondary and currently unserved: puppy owners, senior-dog owners, therapeutic-diet owners, non-values-aligned curious owners

## 3. Recurring pains or friction
- plant-based dog food marketing is heavier on ethics and lighter on nutrition
- "vet approved" and "complete and balanced" phrases are used loosely across the category
- hard to tell which plant-based brands have taken nutritional adequacy seriously and which have not
- owners do not want to choose between values and their dog's nutritional safety
- the AAFCO standard is opaque to most buyers; brands do not explain which flavor of compliance they meet (formulation vs. feeding trial)

## 4. Desired outcomes
- feel confident the food is nutritionally defensible for an adult dog
- see specific, checkable claims rather than lifestyle language
- understand exactly what the food is and is not for (adult maintenance, not puppies, not therapeutic)
- feel that the brand respects the trust question enough to answer it directly

## 5. Trust questions or buying resistance
- is this actually nutritionally complete for my adult dog?
- who formulated it, and what are their credentials?
- is the AAFCO compliance by formulation or by feeding trial?
- has the amino acid profile been verified, not just formulated?
- what has the brand not done that I should know about?
- is my dog's life stage or health condition one this food is appropriate for?

## 6. Native phrases worth keeping
- "formulated by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist"
- "AAFCO adult-maintenance nutrient profile"
- "third-party amino acid testing"
- "for adult dogs"
- "nutritional adequacy"
- "what we have done and what we have not done"

## 7. Generic language to avoid
- "revolutionary pet nutrition"
- "optimize your dog's wellness"
- "sustainable nutrition for modern pet parents"
- "guilt-free feeding"
- "vet approved" used bare
- "clinically proven"
- "healthiest food"
- "feel good about what you feed your dog" (close to native but drifts lifestyle)

## 8. What still feels unknown
- specific palatability experience (no owner feedback corpus yet)
- whether the consulting relationship language will read as strong enough or too hedged
- whether buyers will accept "adult only" as a responsibility signal or read it as a limitation
- what owners of mixed-life-stage households will do (e.g., one adult dog, one puppy)

## 9. Weak claims to avoid early
- "vet approved" in a standalone, un-specified form
- "complete nutrition for any dog"
- "healthier than conventional food"
- "most dogs thrive on Kind Bowl"
- "studies show" references to work Kind Bowl did not do
- outcome claims about individual dogs
- any therapeutic or preventative claim

## Judgment
Strong enough to advance.
The signal set is narrower and more specific than the first-pass signal set, which was correct: the first-pass signal drifted toward "values alignment" framing and underweighted the nutritional-credibility question that the mechanism and proof briefs make central.
