# Page Argument Shape — OpenClaw Web Hosting Homepage

## Page
Homepage — positioning-first opening for developer audience

## What the visitor needs to understand first
That OpenClaw exists to fill a specific gap — not to be another hosting provider, but to solve the problem that lives between easy-but-limited platforms and powerful-but-complex cloud. The visitor needs to recognize their own frustration in the framing before the product is introduced. If the page opens with a product pitch, developers in a saturated category will pattern-match it to the last three hosting providers they evaluated and stop reading.

## What needs to be earned next
Once the gap lands: a clear statement of what OpenClaw is not. In a category full of sameness, ruling out the wrong comparisons is as important as making the right one. Developers carrying post-Heroku lock-in anxiety need to hear "we are not that" before they can hear "we are this."

After the distinction: the specific control claim. Not "powerful" in the abstract — what specifically does a developer own and configure on OpenClaw that they couldn't on Render or Railway? The mechanism must be shown concretely.

After the mechanism: pricing transparency. A worked example or estimator that demonstrates "you will know what this costs before the bill arrives." This is a trust move, not a sales move.

After pricing: portability. The exit question. What happens if you want to leave? What do you own, in what format, and how long does it take? This is the section that earns the most trust with post-Heroku developers and costs nothing to answer honestly.

After trust: proof. Developer case studies that show the specific scenarios the product solves.

## What the argument order should be

1. **The gap** — name the frustration between PaaS and raw cloud; produce recognition
2. **What OpenClaw is not** — rule out the wrong mental models before the product is introduced
3. **The control claim** — specific, concrete: what you own and configure
4. **Pricing transparency** — a demonstration, not a promise
5. **Portability** — the exit question answered plainly
6. **Proof** — developer scenarios with specificity
7. **CTA** — deploy your first app, start a trial

## Section plan

**Section 1 — The gap (hero)**
Open with the developer frustration, not the product name.
Lead line: the recognition moment — outgrowing your platform but not wanting to become a cloud engineer to fix it.
Sub-headline: one sentence on what OpenClaw is — developer hosting with real control, without the DevOps tax.
CTA: deploy in minutes / start free.

**Section 2 — What OpenClaw is not**
Short. Two or three sentences each, no more.
Not a PaaS with prettier UI and the same pricing cliff.
Not a raw VPS that hands you a terminal and good luck.
Not another platform that makes migration a six-day project.
This section is a relief valve — it lets the developer exhale before the product pitch begins.

**Section 3 — The control claim**
What specifically does a developer own and configure on OpenClaw?
Show three or four concrete things:
- Your deployment configuration lives in a file you version alongside your code
- You choose your runtime, your build process, your environment — not a platform default
- Your data and config are exportable on demand, in standard formats
- You can reproduce your environment locally or on any provider
Keep this behavioral — what you do, not what the product "offers."

**Section 4 — Pricing transparency**
A worked example: a typical app (web server, database, some storage, moderate traffic) and what it actually costs per month — not a tier grid, but a specific number with the components shown.
Supporting line: no egress surprises, no feature-gating that forces you up a tier unexpectedly, a cost estimator if available.
This section should feel like a spreadsheet, not a sales pitch.

**Section 5 — Portability**
Answer the exit question directly.
What do you own: your config files, your data, your deployment scripts — in standard formats, not proprietary ones.
How long does migration take: a real estimate, not "easy."
Why this matters: because the last platform didn't make this easy, and they paid for it.
This section is short but it is where the most trust is earned or lost for the post-Heroku reader.

**Section 6 — Proof**
Two or three developer cases with enough specificity to be evidence:
- A post-Heroku migration story: what they were running, what the migration involved, what changed
- A "finally has control" story: a developer who was on Render or Railway, hit the limits, moved to OpenClaw, and got what they needed
- A cost story: what they were paying, what they pay now, with enough detail to be credible
Do not use generic "great hosting, highly recommend" quotes. Scenario-level or it doesn't count.

**Section 7 — CTA**
Clean. One action. Restate the core claim in one line.
Optional: a "see the docs" secondary link for developers who want to evaluate technical fit before committing.

## What sections would be unnecessary or harmful

**Uptime SLA percentage in the hero** — 99.9% is what everyone says. Leading with it signals that the brand has nothing more interesting to say.

**Speed benchmark comparisons** — relevant to evaluation but not to the opening argument. Belongs on a features or comparison page if it exists. On the homepage, it reads as defensive.

**Datacenter location list** — technical detail that belongs in docs. Adds length without adding trust.

**Technology compatibility matrix** — same. The developer who needs to know if their stack is supported will find the docs. The homepage shouldn't become a spec sheet.

**Founder or team story** — save for an About page. Does not do trust work for a skeptical first-time developer visitor.

**"Join X,000 developers" social proof in the hero** — if the number is small, it backfires. If it's large, it only adds credibility after the argument is already working. Don't lead with it.

## Where trust should land
Sections 4 and 5 together — pricing transparency and portability. These are the two trust questions that matter most in this category right now. Section 3 (control) earns product credibility; sections 4 and 5 earn platform credibility. The distinction matters.

## Where proof should land
Section 6, after the trust work is done. A developer story read before the pricing and portability sections would raise the question "okay but what did they pay and can they leave?" — the argument needs to answer those before proof can land cleanly.

## Drafting constraints
- Do not claim a specific uptime percentage without verified data
- Do not give a specific pricing example without confirming it against the real product pricing model
- The portability section must describe real product capabilities — what formats, what export mechanism — not aspirational portability language
- "Focus on your code, not your infrastructure" is banned — it has been said by every host and means nothing
- Do not open with "blazing fast" or "reliable" — category noise
- The "what OpenClaw is not" section must be specific enough to produce recognition, not vague enough to apply to everyone
- Proof section: no invented developer names, project names, or cost numbers — mark as placeholder if real stories are not yet available
