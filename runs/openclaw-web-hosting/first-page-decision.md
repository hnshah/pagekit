# First-Page Decision — OpenClaw Web Hosting

## Candidate first page
Homepage.

## Why this page first
Web hosting is a saturated category. Developers arriving at OpenClaw have seen dozens of providers and their copy is indistinguishable. The homepage's job is to produce a recognition moment — "this understands what I actually need" — fast enough that the developer doesn't bounce before reading the second section.

No other page can do this work first. A pricing page assumes the visitor already believes the product is the right fit. A features page assumes they're past the "is this for me?" question. A migration guide landing page would be high-intent for post-Heroku developers specifically, but the homepage has to set the context that makes the migration guide credible.

The recognition job is not served by a generic hosting pitch. It's served by naming the specific frustration — the PaaS-to-cloud gap, the lock-in anxiety, the DevOps overhead — in language developers recognize from their own experience.

## Page job
Help the developer understand:
1. OpenClaw is built for a specific gap: not shared hosting, not raw cloud, not another PaaS with a different color scheme
2. "Control" means something specific here — what you own, what you can configure, what you can take with you
3. Pricing is transparent and predictable — you will know what you're paying before the bill arrives
4. If you want to leave, you can — and it won't take a week of migration work

And help them do: sign up, start a free trial, or deploy their first app.

## Why this page before other pages
- A migration guide page would serve post-Heroku developers with high intent — but they arrive at the homepage first, and without the homepage framing, the migration guide is just a how-to, not a trust signal
- A pricing page is meaningless before the visitor understands what they're pricing
- A features/docs page assumes developer buy-in that the homepage hasn't yet earned
- A "why OpenClaw" comparison page would be too deep for first contact

## Visitor state on arrival
Three likely arrival states:

**Active evaluator:** Has a specific project or migration in mind. Is comparing OpenClaw against two or three alternatives. Wants to know quickly whether this is worth a closer look. Sensitive to anything that sounds like generic hosting copy.

**Post-Heroku migrant:** Was forced off a platform and is looking for something that won't repeat the experience. Specifically alert to lock-in signals and pricing opacity. Will read the page differently from other visitors — every sentence is being tested against "is this what Heroku said too?"

**Curious developer:** Saw OpenClaw mentioned somewhere, has no immediate hosting need, is evaluating for future reference. Lower stakes visit but forms the brand impression that becomes relevant later.

All three benefit from the same opening: name the real problem, distinguish OpenClaw from the wrong mental models, make the portability claim clearly.

## Main trust burden
Portability and longevity: "If I build on this, can I leave? And will it still be here in two years?" This is the trust question that Heroku's shutdown activated and that post-Heroku developers carry to every new platform evaluation. It needs a real answer — what specifically do you own, in what format, and how long does export take — not a general reassurance.

Secondary trust burden: pricing predictability. "Will I get a surprise bill?" needs to be answered with a mechanism, not a promise.

## Main proof burden
- A specific portability claim: what configuration format do you own, what can you export, what's the migration path off OpenClaw
- A pricing transparency demonstration: a cost estimator or a clear worked example of what a typical app costs, not just a tier table
- Developer case studies with enough specificity to be credible: what they were on before, what they're running now, what changed

## What this decision rules out for now
- Opening with uptime SLA percentages — table stakes, not differentiation
- A speed benchmark section in the first pass — relevant but not trust-building
- An enterprise or team tier pitch — wrong audience for the opening argument
- A "what our customers say" generic testimonial wall — proof must be scenario-level to do trust work
- A technology compatibility matrix in the homepage — belongs on a docs or features page
