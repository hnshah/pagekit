# Message Spine — Verdel

## Core truths

**Truth 1: The nutritional question is real and Verdel has a specific answer to it.**
"Complete and balanced" is a defined standard, not a marketing phrase. Verdel meets AAFCO adult maintenance profiles. The formulation adds taurine, L-carnitine, and algae-derived DHA because plant proteins do not supply them in the same ratios as animal proteins. A board-certified veterinary nutritionist reviewed the recipe before launch. These are the facts; they are the answer to the nutritional question and need to be stated as facts, not as reassurance.

**Truth 2: Verdel is grain-inclusive, and that distinction matters.**
The FDA's investigation into canine dilated cardiomyopathy centred on grain-free, legume-heavy diets. Verdel contains oats and brown rice. This is not a minor formulation detail — it is the direct response to the most ambient safety concern in the vegan dog food category. The page should say this clearly and early, not bury it in an ingredient list.

**Truth 3: The guarantee is the honest answer to the palatability question.**
Verdel has no palatability rate data to cite. What it has is a 30-day money-back guarantee with no return required. That is a more credible answer than "dogs love it" because it acknowledges the real risk and backs it with something actionable. The page should treat the guarantee as a trust mechanism, not a sales footnote.

## Audience distinctions worth preserving

**Values-led owners** need truth 1 confirmed before truth 3 is useful. Once confident the nutrition is sound, the purchase decision follows.

**Health-led / allergy owners** need a path that explains why novel plant proteins may resolve a sensitivity to chicken or beef. A homepage can name this use case without routing to a separate page, provided it is specific enough to produce recognition.

**DCM-concerned visitors** are served by truth 2, but only if it appears early. A grain-inclusive statement buried in a late section will not reach the visitor who arrived with that concern.

## Native language to keep
- "complete and balanced" (with the AAFCO qualifier)
- "grain-inclusive" — not the same as grain-free
- "30-day guarantee" / "no return required"
- "food sensitivity" / "protein allergy"
- "formulated with veterinary nutritionist review"

## Language to drop
- "guilt-free" — treats the owner as needing absolution
- "vet approved" — overstates the DACVN relationship
- "dogs love it" — no data; the guarantee makes this claim unnecessary
- "planet-friendly" — no certification in place

## Claims to test later
- Whether stating "grain-inclusive" in the hero section (DCM reassurance) changes conversion relative to leading with the AAFCO claim
- Whether the guarantee as the primary palatability answer outperforms a success-rate claim (which does not exist) for the values-led audience

## What this should change downstream

**First-page decision:** The three truths all address trust rather than brand discovery. This suggests the first page should be built around answering the safety question, not announcing the brand. A non-homepage option ("is this safe for my dog?") should be evaluated seriously at step 03.

**Proof choices:** Confirmed proof assets: AAFCO status, DACVN review, 30-day guarantee. No testimonials, no palatability data, no longitudinal studies. The draft must stay within these limits.

**Language choices:** State facts, not claims. "Verdel meets AAFCO complete-and-balanced standards for adult maintenance" is a fact. "Verdel gives your dog everything they need" is a claim. Use the former.
