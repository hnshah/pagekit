# First-Page Decision — Me CRM

## Candidates considered

### Candidate A: Homepage

A homepage for Me CRM could open with the recognition work the spine calls for — naming the "gone dark" failure mode prominently in the hero, then moving through product distinction and maintenance doubt in sequence. It has the structural flexibility to hold all three message jobs without fragmenting the visitor across multiple pages. A homepage is also the most defensible choice for a pre-launch product with no established acquisition channels: it serves every visitor type (curious, skeptical, referred) from a single URL, without assuming the visitor arrived via a specific search intent. For a product at early stage, the homepage may be the only page that realistically exists, and building it well is more valuable than building a specialized page for a visitor segment that hasn't materialized.

### Candidate B: Problem-first landing page

A problem-first landing page built around the recognition job — naming the failure mode specifically and only then introducing the product — could outperform a homepage for founders who arrive already frustrated. This page would not open with the product name or category. It would open with the experience: the inbox search, the three threads, the context that has already faded, the message you put off again. By the time Me CRM is named, the visitor has recognized themselves in the description. This structure may convert a self-aware-problem founder better than a homepage because it meets them at the exact emotional state where a new tool feels worth trying. It doesn't require the founder to make a categorical leap ("I need a CRM"); it requires only recognition ("that is exactly what happens to me").

### Candidate C: "What Me CRM is not" category-explainer page

A category explainer built around distinction — what Me CRM is not (not Salesforce, not a pipeline, not a spreadsheet) — could work well for founders who have already tried the category-default tools and found them wrong for the job. This page would acknowledge prior disappointment explicitly: "You've tried putting it in HubSpot. You've tried a Notion table. You know the problem is real. This is different." For a product whose clearest proposition is what it refuses to be, a distinction-forward page could do sharp work with a founder who has already cycled through alternatives. The argument from negation is strongest when the audience shares a prior failed tool — a real sub-segment of the target audience.

### Candidate D: Specific-scenario page ("The investor you haven't updated in three months")

A scenario-specific page built around one high-stakes relationship failure — the investor who has gone dark, or the advisor who hasn't heard from you in six months — would be maximally specific about one of the most emotionally loaded versions of the problem. It would speak directly to the fundraising context where relationship drift has the clearest consequences, and small enough to do one job very well. This candidate is strong for any founder who arrives via a specific search or referral context related to investor relationship management. The scenario is vivid; the consequence is clear; the problem is named with precision.

## Decision: Candidate B — Problem-first landing page

The message spine has one primary job before anything else: recognition. The trust question the page must answer first is not "what does this product do" but "does this product understand the problem I have?" A homepage could do recognition work, but it would have to compete with the category signals ("CRM," "manage your relationships") that trigger comparison-shopping behavior before the founder has acknowledged the problem is real for them.

A problem-first landing page removes the category noise entirely. It opens in the experience — the inbox search, the context that has already faded — and earns the right to introduce the product only after the visitor has said yes to the problem. This structure is specifically suited to a product whose wedge is experiential (the specific failure mode of relationship drift) rather than categorical (a new kind of CRM).

## Confidence basis

Hypothesis. Inferred from the message spine and product brief; not validated by traffic data, search intent analysis, or user interviews. A testable first-page bet.

## Why this page beats the others for Me CRM

Homepage (A) carries category-default signals. The moment "CRM" appears in a hero, the visitor's frame shifts to comparison-shopping. A problem-first page defers category labeling until after recognition has happened — something a homepage structure cannot easily do without losing the visitors who need the label to orient. Category-explainer (C) starts with the category rather than the founder's experience; it assumes the visitor has already tried an alternative and found it wrong, which is only a sub-segment. Scenario-specific (D) is too narrow as a first page — it serves investors but underserves founders whose critical relationships are with advisors, customers, or key hires.

## Page job

Help a founder who has experienced relationship drift recognize that the experience is a solvable problem, and that Me CRM is built specifically for it.

## Visitor state on arrival

A founder who has felt the problem — follow-up debt accumulating, context fading after meetings, a specific relationship that has gone quieter than it should. They are in "I should deal with this" mode, not comparison-shopping mode. They may not have a product category in mind at all.

## Main trust burden

Maintenance doubt: "Will I actually keep this updated, or is this another tool I set up and abandon in two weeks?"

## Main proof burden

Specificity of the problem description. The page earns trust not through testimonials (none available) but through how precisely it names the failure mode. Accurate problem description makes the product claim more credible.

## What this decision rules out for now

A features-first page. A pricing-forward page. A category-comparison page. A founder origin story as the opening argument. These may all be useful later pages; none should be first.
