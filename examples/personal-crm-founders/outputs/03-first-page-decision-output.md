### Candidates considered

**Candidate A: Homepage**
A homepage for Me CRM could open with the recognition work the spine calls for — naming the "gone dark" failure mode prominently in the hero, then moving through product distinction and maintenance doubt in sequence. It has the structural flexibility to hold all three message jobs without fragmenting the visitor across multiple pages. A homepage is also the most defensible choice for a pre-launch product with no existing traffic or segment-specific acquisition channels established: it serves every visitor type (curious, skeptical, referred) from a single URL, without assuming the visitor arrived via a specific search intent. For a product at early stage, the homepage may be the only page that realistically exists, and building it well is more valuable than building a specialized page for a visitor segment that hasn't yet materialized.

**Candidate B: Problem-first landing page ("You know you should follow up")**
A problem-first landing page built around the recognition job — naming the failure mode specifically and only then introducing the product — could outperform a homepage for founders who arrive already frustrated with the problem. This page would not open with the product name or category. It would open with the experience: the inbox search, the three threads, the context that's already faded, the message you put off. By the time Me CRM is named, the visitor has already recognized themselves in the description. This structure may convert a self-aware-problem founder better than a homepage because it meets them at the exact emotional state that makes a new tool feel worth trying. It doesn't require the founder to make a categorical leap ("I need a CRM"); it only requires recognition ("that is exactly what happens to me").

**Candidate C: "What Me CRM is not" category-explainer page**
A category explainer built around distinction — what Me CRM is not (not Salesforce, not a pipeline, not a spreadsheet) — could be powerful for founders who have already tried the category-default tools and found them wrong for the job. This page would acknowledge the prior disappointment explicitly: "You've tried putting it in HubSpot. You've tried a Notion table. You know the problem is real. This is different." For a product whose clearest selling proposition is what it refuses to be, a distinction-forward page could do sharp work with a founder who has already cycled through alternatives. The risk is that it starts with the category (CRM, tools, alternatives) rather than the founder's experience, which may lose founders who haven't yet tried a category solution and don't have a prior product to contrast against.

**Candidate D: Specific-scenario page ("The investor you haven't updated in three months")**
A scenario-specific page built around one high-stakes relationship failure — the investor who has gone dark, or the advisor who hasn't heard from you in six months — would be maximally specific about one of the most emotionally loaded versions of the problem. It would speak directly to the fundraising context where relationship drift has the clearest consequences, and it would be small enough to do one job very well. The candidate is strong for any founder who arrives via a specific search or referral context ("CRM for managing investor relationships"). The limitation: it narrows the audience before the visitor has self-selected. Solo founders, operators, and founders whose most critical relationships are with customers or advisors rather than investors would find the page partially relevant but not maximally so.

### Decision: Candidate B — Problem-first landing page

The message spine has one primary job before anything else: recognition. The trust question the page must answer first is not "what does this product do" but "does this product understand the problem I have?" Founders who have experienced follow-up debt and context fade are not looking for a CRM; they're looking for something that makes the specific experience stop. A homepage could do recognition work, but it would have to compete with the category signals ("CRM," "manage your relationships") that trigger comparison-shopping behavior before the founder has acknowledged the problem is real for them.

A problem-first landing page removes the category noise entirely. It opens in the experience — the inbox search, the context that has already faded — and earns the right to introduce the product only after the visitor has said yes to the problem. This structure is specifically suited to a product whose wedge is experiential (the specific failure mode of relationship drift) rather than categorical (a new kind of CRM).

The confidence basis is hypothesis: this is a pattern-level inference from the message spine, not traffic data. It is a testable bet.

### Candidates rejected

**Homepage (Candidate A):** Rejected because a homepage's structural openness also means it carries category-default signals. The moment "CRM" appears in a hero, the visitor's frame shifts to comparison-shopping (versus Notion, Salesforce, HubSpot). A problem-first page can defer category labeling until after recognition has happened. A homepage cannot easily do this without losing the visitors who need the category label to orient. Additionally, the homepage structure tends to sequence: hero, features, social proof, pricing — which puts recognition work in the hero only, with the product taking over immediately after. The spine calls for recognition to dominate the early sections before distinction work begins.

**Category-explainer (Candidate C):** Rejected because it starts with the category (CRM, tools, alternatives) rather than the founder's experience. A founder who has not tried Salesforce for relationships, or who hasn't yet reached for a tool category at all, will find this page partially resonant but not maximally so. The product's argument is strongest when it starts from the experience, not from the product category it replaces.

**Scenario-specific page (Candidate D):** Rejected because it narrows the scope before the visitor has self-selected. Investors are one of several high-stakes relationship types (advisors, early customers, key hires, press) and a page built around investor relationship management would underserve founders whose most critical relationships fall into other categories. The scenario is vivid but too narrow for a first page; it is a strong later page for a specific acquisition channel.

### Page job
Help a founder who has experienced relationship drift — the specific failure of going dark on someone who mattered, or reaching out without knowing where you left things — recognize that the experience is a solvable problem and that Me CRM is built specifically for it.

### Visitor state on arrival
A founder who has felt the problem: follow-up debt accumulating, context fading after meetings, a specific relationship that has gone quieter than it should have. They are not in comparison-shopping mode yet. They may not have a product category in mind at all. They are in "I should deal with this" mode.

### Main trust burden
Maintenance doubt. "Will I actually keep this updated, or is this another tool I set up and abandon in two weeks?"

### Main proof burden
Specificity of the problem description. The page earns trust not through testimonials (none available) but through how precisely it names the failure mode the founder has experienced. If the description of the problem is specific and accurate, the product claim is more credible.

### What this decision rules out for now
A features-first page. A pricing-forward page. A category-comparison page. A founder origin story page. These may all be useful downstream pages; none of them should be first.

### Confidence basis
Hypothesis. Inferred from the message spine and the product brief; not validated by traffic data, search intent analysis, or user interviews. This is a testable first-page bet.
