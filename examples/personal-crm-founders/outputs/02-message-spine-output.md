## Raw output (as produced)

### 1. Core truth

The problem founders have is not too many contacts — it's that the relationships that matter most accumulate unanswered questions: when did we last talk, what did I promise, what's the next step? Me CRM is a place to track that state, for the 50–100 relationships that actually drive the company, without the overhead of a sales tool built for a different job.

### 2. Target audience

Early-stage startup founders (seed to Series A) who manage high-stakes relationship portfolios alone or with one co-founder. These are founders who have already felt the specific failure: realizing they've gone dark on an investor, or reaching out to an advisor without knowing where they left things.

### 3. Sharp pain statement

You know you should follow up. You don't remember exactly what you said last time, what you promised, or how long it's been. You search your inbox. You find three threads. You still don't have the full picture. You either send a vague message or you put it off again.

### 4. Desired shift or outcome

A founder who uses Me CRM can look at any important relationship and immediately know: last contact date, what was discussed, what they owe, and when to reach back out. The pile of "I should follow up" has an external home. Relationships don't go dark without a prompt.

### 5. Strongest language to preserve

- "gone dark" — name the failure mode
- "where you are with someone" — the specific state the product tracks
- "follow-up debt" — the accumulated pile
- "the 50 relationships that matter" — scope that distinguishes from sales CRM volume
- "context fade" — what happens between conversations

### 6. Page-level message implications

- The first job is **recognition**: a founder who has experienced this problem needs to see it named accurately before they care about the solution. If the page starts with the product, it loses the founders who haven't yet identified their problem as a solvable one.
- The second job is **product distinction**: Me CRM is not another CRM. The specific "no pipeline, no deals, no lead scoring" cuts matter more than any feature list.
- The third job is **trust / maintenance doubt**: the skeptical concern ("will I actually keep this updated?") is real and should be addressed directly, not smoothed over.

### 7. Key trust question to answer

"Is this something I will actually use, or will it be another tool I set up and abandon?"

This is not a data-privacy question first (though that matters). It is a usage-pattern question. The founder's deeper concern is that the problem is real but their behavior is the bottleneck — they know they don't maintain systems consistently. The page must address the adoption concern directly.

### 8. Weak directions to avoid

- Starting with features before recognition: a feature list before the pain is named loses the audience
- Leading with the founder origin story before the user's problem: the "built by a founder" origin is real but secondary — it earns trust, it does not create need
- "CRM" as the primary category label in the hero: positions the product next to Salesforce, Notion, HubSpot — all wrong comparisons
- Soft language ("stay connected," "keep relationships warm") that does not name the specific failure mode
- A pricing-forward page: price follows trust; a founder hasn't committed to the problem being worth solving yet
